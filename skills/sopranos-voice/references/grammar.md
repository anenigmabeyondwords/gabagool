# Grammar

This file is the grammar of the dialect: the shape of its sentences and every construction that departs from standard written English, with how often each one turns up, who uses it and what it does. It was built from a full read of every episode, 86 in all, by readers who logged constructions episode by episode, plus pattern and word counts run over the whole subtitle corpus of 437,410 words. It contains no quoted dialogue: every example sentence is invented, and transcript words appear only as single forms of three words or fewer.

## Sentence shape

**Length.** Sentences are short. The median sentence is 5 words and the mean is 6.07; 55.6% of sentences are five words or fewer, and only 1.9% run to twenty words or more. The corpus holds 52,258 subtitle lines. The transcripts carry no speaker labels, so every per-line figure here is per subtitle line, not per speaker turn.

**Fragments.** A large share of turns are not full clauses at all. Readers recorded verbless turns (a bare noun phrase, adjective, price, place or list standing as the whole answer) in 58 episodes, and dropped subjects in all 86. Reports, verdicts, offers, excuses and relayed phone messages come out as strings of clipped pieces, and a chain of fragments can carry a whole story or a life history.

**Questions.** 23% of subtitle lines contain a question (question rate 0.23). Most questions are reduced: the helping verb is dropped, statement word order carries a rising tone, or a profane infix sits after the wh-word. After *i don't know* (587), the most frequent three-word sequences in the corpus are question openers: *what are you* (403), *what do you* (375) and *what the fuck* (338). Many questions are not requests for information: they accuse, mock, check agreement or answer themselves.

**Long turns.** Long sentences are the exception and belong to particular moments. The readers place them in therapy, in childhood stories and anecdotes, in tirades and grievance lists, in bargaining and in grief, where turns run on through chains of *and*, *and then*, *because* and *you know*. Carmela, Melfi and Meadow keep fuller, more standard sentences than the men; Paulie and Janice hold the floor with long looping stories in the present tense.

**Repetition.** Speakers repeat to insist rather than to clarify. Readers recorded words, commands and interjections said two or three times in a row in 7 episodes, and the character notes show the habit at larger scale: a boss repeating an order or a decision word for word until the plea dies, a refusal repeated rather than explained, an echo of the other person's word thrown back as a question.

## Constructions

Sections run from most to least common. Two kinds of number appear. A rate per 1,000 words comes from the corpus statistics, which counted a fixed set of patterns over all 437,410 words; a pattern only catches the forms it can see, so a rate is a floor. "Episodes: N" comes from the season notes, where readers recorded constructions in each of the 86 episodes; the count adds up, season by season, the episodes in which a construction was logged; where one season logged it under two names the larger count is used, and where a season mentions it only inside a broader entry, that season counts as one episode. The order weighs both: the measured rate where there is one, the episode spread where there is not. Who uses a construction comes from the notes.

### Questions without the helping verb

- **Pattern:** (wh-word) + subject + bare verb, -ing form, participle, *gonna*, *got* or predicate, with *do*, *be* or *have* left out and no inversion. In quick offers and checks the subject goes too, leaving a bare verb or noun phrase with a rising tone.
- **How often:** rate 1.17 per 1,000 words for *you* + *want*, *need*, *think* and similar verbs asked with no *do* (count 512); 0.47 per 1,000 for subject + -ing questions (count 206). Episodes: 86 in the notes, every episode.
- **Who:** the default question shape for the whole cast, densest in Tony, Junior and Richie, and common in Paulie, Christopher and Carmela. Federal agents, doctors, a priest and now and then the therapist use it too, so readers treat it as broad casual speech rather than a crew marker.
- **What it does:** carries greetings and how-are-you checks, offers of food, drink and rides, logistics, status checks and hostile challenges. With a third-person subject the verb stays bare, since the missing helper carried the ending. Right after a profane infix, the same shape turns a greeting into a demand for an account.
- **Example:** *You eating with us, or you still fixing that build?*

### Got for have

- **Pattern:** subject + *got* + noun phrase for possession, availability, ailments or existence, with *have* or *has* left out. Questions take the shape *you got* + noun phrase? with no *do*; *got no* + noun says someone has none; *you got* or *they got* + noun phrase can stand for *there is*.
- **How often:** rate 0.20 per 1,000 words for *you got* questions with no *do* (count 88) and 0.09 per 1,000 for *got no* + noun (count 40), both narrow slices of the construction. As a word, *got* (1,990) is as frequent as *have* (1,991) and far ahead of *has* (326). Episodes: 86 in the notes, every episode.
- **Who:** nearly every native speaker. Tony leans on it most, especially when boasting or pleading; Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Carmela, Johnny Sack and Bobby use it constantly, and agents pick it up when they slip into street register.
- **What it does:** the everyday verb of having: money, debts, children, symptoms, appointments, weapons, contacts, grudges, what a shop has in stock and what someone is owed. It sets up offers and excuses, and in question form it can become a challenge about whether there is trouble between two people. The full *have* is rare in casual talk.
- **Example:** *We got two servers down and you got the only key?*

### Left dislocation

- **Pattern:** noun phrase, name, list or pronoun set out first, + a comment clause in which a pronoun or demonstrative points back to it. A hanging-topic variant has no pronoun copy at all; a stronger variant gives the topic its own utterance; a fronted *me* sets the speaker apart; the comment can be a dismissive order.
- **How often:** rate 0.12 per 1,000 words for a sentence-initial *this*, *that*, *my* or *the* + noun picked up by a pronoun (count 51); 0.05 per 1,000 with a possessive such as *your* or *his* up front (count 20); 0.01 per 1,000 for a fronted *me* followed by *I* (count 4). Episodes: 86 in the notes, every episode.
- **Who:** nearly every regular speaker, from Tony, Carmela, Junior and Christopher to Janice, Paulie, Silvio and Artie. Readers found it especially heavy in the English of the Italian visitors and other second-language speakers.
- **What it does:** names the person, group, place, sum or grievance as a headline, then rules on it. It carries gossip, judgments of people, deal terms, agenda items at a sit-down, item-by-item rulings and stories. A run of items can be collapsed into a single *it* or *that*; the hanging version marks a turn to a delicate subject.
- **Example:** *That printer on the third floor, it eats a page every morning.*

### Subject drop

- **Pattern:** finite verb, modal or predicate opening a clause with *I*, *it*, *he*, *we*, *you* or *there* left out, often with the helping verb gone too, frequently in a run of short clauses.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 86 in the notes, every episode.
- **Who:** everyone. Tony, Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Carmela and Silvio use it constantly; clinicians, a federal agent, a nurse and a pastor drop subjects as freely as the crew, so readers read it as brisk spoken economy.
- **What it does:** keeps reports, recaps, guesses, excuses, offers, verdicts and leave-takings clipped, and keeps the focus on the event rather than the actor. It drives relayed phone messages and narration, a cover story can be built almost entirely from subjectless clauses, and stock openers and sign-offs lose their first words. The missing subject is always clear from context.
- **Example:** *Called the vendor. Says the part ships Monday. Doubt it.*

### Expletive as all-purpose intensifier

- **Pattern:** *fucking* or *fuckin'* (also *goddamn*, *motherfuckin'*, and the milder *friggin'* and *freakin'*) before a noun, adjective, verb, number or name; between a determiner and its noun; between a first name and a surname; inside a fixed phrase; stacked; or as a filler before a whole clause.
- **How often:** rate 5.9 per 1,000 words (count 2,581), the most frequent pattern the statistics measured. The spelling *fuckin'* alone runs at 2.22 per 1,000 (count 971). Episodes: 82 in the notes.
- **Who:** near universal among the men: Tony, Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Silvio, Johnny Sack, Phil. Women and professionals use it mostly at breaking points: Carmela when furious, the therapist under pressure. Staff, a hotel clerk and some of the women avoid it, and it nearly vanishes in a school office.
- **What it does:** marks heat rather than meaning and lands on whichever word carries the stress, in anger, contempt, grief, awe, praise and even thanks. Before a first name it brands someone as a standing irritation; it stacks in tirades and itemized complaints, and it rises with anger and drink.
- **Example:** *The whole fucking release, held up by one fucking semicolon.*

### Dropped g

- **Pattern:** -*ing* said and spelled -*in'*, on verbs and on *nothing* and *something*.
- **How often:** rate 5.4 per 1,000 words for any word ending in -*in'* before a space or punctuation (count 2,364); a narrower first-pass pattern counted 0.10 per 1,000 (count 44). The season notes keep it in their spelling lists rather than among constructions, so there is no episode count.
- **Who:** the statistics cannot say, since lines carry no speaker labels.
- **What it does:** marks casual speech on the most common verbs: *doin'* (130), *goin'* (109), *talkin'* (96), *nothin'* (68), *somethin'* (67). The full forms remain more common; *doing* alone appears 519 times.
- **Example:** *We were fixin' it all mornin' and nothin' changed.*

### Gonna, wanna and oughta

- **Pattern:** (*be*, often dropped) + *gonna* + bare verb for the future; *wanna* + bare verb; *oughta* + bare verb; the stacked *be* + *gonna have to* + verb. Once the reduced form stands for *going* in its motion sense.
- **How often:** *gonna* 3.32 per 1,000 words (count 1,452); *wanna* 0.98 per 1,000 (count 427); *oughta* 0.05 per 1,000 (count 23). Episodes: 16 in the notes, where most readers treated it as spelling rather than logging it as a construction.
- **Who:** every member of the family and nearly everyone else: Tony, Carmela, A.J., Christopher, Ralph. The full *going to* turns up mainly in careful speech and from second-language speakers; one episode's subtitles mostly spell it out, so the reduced form is under-recorded there.
- **What it does:** the default way to state plans, predictions, threats and refusals. *Wanna* carries desires, invitations and offers; *oughta* floats advice, softly from the therapist and sarcastically from a crew member. The stacked *gonna have to* softens a demand into regretted necessity.
- **Example:** *We're gonna have to roll it back, and nobody's gonna like it.*

### Confirmation tags: huh, right, eh

- **Pattern:** statement, question, order or fragment + *huh?*, *right?*, *eh?*, *okay?*, *yeah?*, *hmm?*, *see?* or *you know?*
- **How often:** rate 2.81 per 1,000 words for a closing *right*, *huh*, *okay*, *you know*, *no* or similar with a question mark (count 1,228); 0.07 per 1,000 for *eh*, *yeah*, *hmm* or *see* tacked onto a word (count 32). Episodes: 55 in the notes.
- **Who:** constant in Tony's speech and common everywhere: Christopher, Carmela, Paulie, Junior, Janice, Artie, Johnny Sack. Second-language speakers use *yes* and *no* as tags.
- **What it does:** *huh* is by far the most frequent. It invites agreement in small talk, needles, rubs in a point, softens an order or a goodbye, makes a leering remark sound like small talk, and can be repeated right through an interrogation. *Right* checks a shared assumption, a rumor or a fear, walks a listener through a scheme step by step or leads a witness. *Eh* and *yeah* close farewells, toasts and holiday wishes; *okay* turns an order into a checked agreement.
- **Example:** *Nice work on the migration, huh? And you backed it up first, right?*

### Gotta for obligation

- **Pattern:** subject (often dropped) + *gotta*, *got to* or *'ve gotta* + bare verb or passive, with *have* left out. Negative *don't gotta*; epistemic *it's gotta be* or *that has gotta* + predicate for a confident guess.
- **How often:** the spelling *gotta* runs at 1.47 per 1,000 words (count 644), and *i gotta* is among the hundred most frequent two-word sequences (count 323). The epistemic *gotta be* runs at 0.05 per 1,000 (count 21). Episodes: 82 in the notes.
- **Who:** nearly everyone: Tony, Christopher, Carmela, Paulie, Junior, Bobby, Meadow, Janice, A.J. It reaches a doctor and a dying man. The full *have to* is rare by comparison.
- **What it does:** states duty, need and urgency, and is especially frequent in leave-taking and bodily needs. The same form handles a dinner errand and the decision that a man has to go. The negative waves off what someone thinks they must do; with the subject dropped it becomes a reasoning tag that pushes for agreement; the epistemic use makes confident guesses about a price, a place or a state.
- **Example:** *Gotta reboot the router. That's gotta be what's dropping the calls.*

### The fuck after a wh-word

- **Pattern:** wh-word (+ contracted helper) + *the fuck*, *the hell* or *the heck* + rest of the question, also inside embedded and relative clauses; *whatever the fuck* or *wherever the fuck* as a free relative.
- **How often:** rate 1.28 per 1,000 words (count 558); *what the fuck* alone is the fourth most frequent three-word sequence (count 338). Episodes: 49 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony for his angriest questions, Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Richie and Pussy; A.J., Silvio and Janice too. Carmela prefers *the hell*.
- **What it does:** turns a plain question into accusation, alarm, outrage or disbelief, often as the first line of a confrontation and even as a greeting to someone unexpected. Husband and wife stack rhetorical questions on it in fights, and *whatever the fuck* waves away a word the speaker does not care about.
- **Example:** *Where the hell did the staging database go?*

### Get-passive

- **Pattern:** *get*, *got* or *gotten* + past participle for something done to the subject, the doer usually unnamed; *get* + adjective for a change of state. A related agentless passive with *be* or *have to be* covers violence.
- **How often:** rate 0.85 per 1,000 words (count 370). Episodes: 9 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, Paulie, Christopher, A.J., and the agents who handle informants.
- **What it does:** reports misfortune with the doer in the background: being shot, arrested, jumped, beaten up at school, blamed, drugged, sent to war, struck by a falling pole. With an adjective it marks someone going crazy, angry or dizzy. The agentless version reports or plans a killing without naming who, and states the rule that wives and children are off limits.
- **Example:** *My laptop got stolen and the whole team got locked out.*

### Shit as a catch-all noun

- **Pattern:** *this*, *that*, *some*, *all this* or *the* + (modifier) + *shit*, or noun + *shit*, standing for stuff, matters, talk, gear or a whole domain; *that's some* + adjective + *shit*; noun + *shit like that*.
- **How often:** rate 0.82 per 1,000 words (count 357). Episodes: 28 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Paulie and the crew; Tony B, Little Carmine and Janice. Even the psychiatrist uses it once in a heated speech.
- **What it does:** lumps a topic, scheme, genre, set of rules or whole way of life into one disposable mass noun, usually dismissive, sometimes admiring or simply serious. Tony also uses noun-plus-*shit* labels as bland cover for absences and calls.
- **Example:** *All this config shit, nobody reads it anyway.*

### General extenders

- **Pattern:** list, clause or noun + a vague tail: *or something*, *or whatever*, *and stuff*, *and all*, *and all that*, *and shit*, *or some shit*, *and whatnot*, *and everything*, a spoken *et cetera*, or a prefix + *-whatevers*.
- **How often:** rate 0.42 per 1,000 words (count 184). Episodes: 38 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Bobby, Paulie, A.J., Carmela, Adriana, Ralph and the therapist.
- **What it does:** waves off details the speaker cannot or will not supply: a family origin, a date, a technical word, a list of errands, ethnicity, what was told to the agents. The *and all* version softens a delicate point or skips over trouble; the cruder versions dismiss; a spoken *et cetera* makes a sympathy message sound perfunctory. Once it makes a proposed arson sound casual.
- **Example:** *He wants dashboards, alerts and whatnot, by Friday or something.*

### Free-standing because-clause

- **Pattern:** *'Cause*, *because* or *cos* + full clause, said as its own sentence or turn with no main clause in front; once *because* + a wh-question.
- **How often:** rate 0.42 per 1,000 words (count 184). Episodes: 6 in the notes, once in each season up to the first half of the last.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Artie, Carmela, Junior, Adriana, Johnny Sack, Paulie and Phil.
- **What it does:** supplies a reason after the main point or before anyone asks for one, often to build the speaker's own case. It deflects by answering a different question than the one put, undercuts someone else's version of events, or trails off with no main clause at all. As a bare question after *because*, the reason becomes a challenge the listener has to answer.
- **Example:** *'Cause nobody told me the tests were broken, that's why.*

### Go or come + bare verb

- **Pattern:** *go*, *come* or *coming by* + bare verb, with *and* or *to* left out; sometimes a further verb phrase is chained on, or a motion phrase is followed by a bare verb of purpose.
- **How often:** rate 0.39 per 1,000 words (count 172). Episodes: 22 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Carmela, Janice, A.J., Christopher and Junior; a nurse, a waiter and a police chief too.
- **What it does:** packs errands, orders to children, invitations, dismissals, referrals and leave-taking into one verb chain: barked orders to a driver, plans for lunch, pickups of money, a call to come and sit.
- **Example:** *Go get the charger, then come look at this error.*

### Prefatory What, as a challenge

- **Pattern:** *What,* + echo fragment, guessed proposition, yes/no question, declarative or address term, said with a rising tone; also *What, are you* + guess, *What is this,* + absurd label, and *what,* + subject + *should* + verb?
- **How often:** rate 0.39 per 1,000 words (count 169). Episodes: 33 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Carmela constantly with family, then Paulie, Christopher, Junior, A.J., Artie and Johnny Sack.
- **What it does:** signals disbelief, scorn or mock innocence before the real question. It restates the other person's premise in its most unflattering form, launches a sarcastic guess about a motive, prods a hesitating listener, knocks down an alibi, or buys time under accusation.
- **Example:** *What, you think I deleted the branch on purpose?*

### Fuck + object as a dismissal

- **Pattern:** sentence-initial *fuck* + noun phrase, pronoun, *it*, *that* or what-clause, often repeated in parallel.
- **How often:** rate 0.38 per 1,000 words (count 166). Episodes: 4 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher, also Junior, Artie, Bobby, Paulie and Carmela.
- **What it does:** writes off a person, a superior's wishes, a chore, a sport, a bad hand, family loyalty or a whole room of mourners. With *it*, the speaker quits; with *you*, it is a parting insult; Carmela reports it as the moment she stopped judging.
- **Example:** *Fuck the style guide, ship it.*

### Canonical tag questions

- **Pattern:** statement + auxiliary + pronoun? as a tag, including *ain't it?*, *ain't I?*, *don't it?* and *isn't it?*; once a formal uncontracted *are you not?*
- **How often:** rate 0.36 per 1,000 words (count 159). Episodes: 4 in the notes, three of them mentions inside broader entries on *ain't*, *don't* and sentence-final tags.
- **Who:** spread thin across the cast. The *ain't* tags turn up among the crew, Tony uses a third-person *don't it?* when showing off property, an occasional *isn't it* closes a statement in the last episodes, and the full *are you not?* comes from a saleswoman.
- **What it does:** coaxes agreement, often proudly or ruefully (about a horse, about retirement); the uncontracted version is more formal than anything the men around it say.
- **Example:** *That's a clean deploy, ain't it?*

### Conditional without if

- **Pattern:** condition clause in the plain present (often *you* + present, an imperative or a question), + result clause, with no *if* or *then*; also *ever* + verb as the condition, *you want* + noun? + advice, and an if-less past counterfactual.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 72 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Junior, Carmela, Paulie and Johnny Sack; boys and grown men alike.
- **What it does:** the workhorse frame for threats, warnings, deal terms, ultimatums, house rules, sales pitches and how-the-world-works explanations, and equally for offers and standing invitations. Tony lays out chains of command and habits one clause per step. Stating the condition as flat fact makes the outcome sound already settled.
- **Example:** *You push to main without a review, you're buying lunch for a month.*

### The fuck inside a verb phrase

- **Pattern:** verb (+ object) + *the fuck* or *the hell* + particle, adverb or path phrase; *way the fuck* + direction + place; *don't* + *fucking* + verb.
- **How often:** rate 0.35 per 1,000 words (count 151). Episodes: 16 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Janice, Carmela and A.J.
- **What it does:** splits a phrasal verb to sharpen orders to leave, sit, back off, calm down, get up or grow up, and to stress disbelief or a demand to be left alone. Tony can stretch it across a long list of objects before the closing particle; the path version exaggerates how remote a place is.
- **Example:** *Get the hell off the prod server.*

### Negative concord

- **Pattern:** negated verb, *never* or *ain't* + a second negative word (*nothing*, *nobody*, *no* + noun, *no more*, *no place*, *none*, *neither*, *hardly*) in the same clause, with a single negative meaning; also a clause-final *neither*.
- **How often:** rate 0.29 per 1,000 words for a negator followed within a few words by a second negative (count 127); under 0.01 per 1,000 with *neither* or *hardly* (count 2). Episodes: 68 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Paulie most, then Christopher, Bobby, Adriana, Junior, Phil and Vito; also a priest, the older generation and second-language speakers.
- **What it does:** clusters where feeling runs high: denials, pleas of innocence, refusals, rules laid down, banishments and threats. Tony reaches for it when cornered or when closing a door for good. It adds weight and never cancels, and the same speakers use single negation elsewhere.
- **Example:** *I didn't touch nothing on that server, not nothing.*

### Ain't

- **Pattern:** *ain't* for *isn't*, *aren't*, *am not*, *hasn't*, *haven't* or *there isn't*, before an adjective, noun phrase, -ing form, participle, *gonna* or *got*; once *ain't* + bare verb for *didn't*.
- **How often:** rate 0.29 per 1,000 words (count 129); *ain't got* 0.01 per 1,000 (count 3). Episodes: 52 in the notes.
- **Who:** Paulie, Tony and Christopher most, then Silvio, Junior and Bobby; street dealers, a carjacker, working men on job sites and the old films on television.
- **What it does:** flat, final statements of what is not so: defiant refusals, moral verdicts, dismissive predictions, denials of informing, a parting threat. Tony saves it for grave statements and puts it in another man's mouth when parodying him; it often comes stacked with a second negative.
- **Example:** *That fix ain't gonna hold past Tuesday.*

### Right dislocation

- **Pattern:** clause with a pronoun or vague subject + the full noun phrase it refers to, added at the end: often *this* or *that* + noun, a name, a pitying or scornful tag, or a trailing hedge.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words for a closing *that* or *this* + person noun, or a *poor* + noun tag (count 25). Episodes: 64 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Junior, Christopher, Carmela and Paulie, then Bobby, Pussy, Ralph and the therapist.
- **What it does:** lands the verdict first and names the target last, often with a contemptuous epithet, a sneer or a pitying label; also fond admiration and gossip. It can hold back the real subject so a shady pitch sounds casual, and Carmela uses it to keep an awkward fact about her husband at arm's length.
- **Example:** *He never answers his email, that guy.*

### You guys and you people

- **Pattern:** *you guys* (also *guys*, rarely *y'all*) as the second-person plural, with possessives built on the whole phrase; *you people* as a hostile plural.
- **How often:** *you guys* 0.27 per 1,000 words (count 119); *you people* 0.06 per 1,000 (count 26). Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** A.J. and Tony most; Meadow, Janice, Artie, Christopher and college students too.
- **What it does:** neutral or friendly address to pairs and groups, including both parents at once; A.J. uses it whenever he blames his parents or asks them for something. *You people* turns dismissive in an outburst and gets mocked by being echoed back.
- **Example:** *Are you guys done with the conference room?*

### Fused 'em and 'im

- **Pattern:** a verb written run together with a reduced object pronoun: verb + *'em* or *'im* with no space.
- **How often:** rate 0.26 per 1,000 words (count 113). The season notes keep it in their spelling lists rather than among constructions, so there is no episode count.
- **Who:** the statistics cannot say, since lines carry no speaker labels.
- **What it does:** shows the object pronoun reduced to an unstressed syllable glued to the verb, mostly in quick orders and reports. The free-standing *'em* is a separate spelling (count 80).
- **Example:** *Tell'em the meeting moved to four.*

### Object pronoun in a coordinated subject

- **Pattern:** *me*, *him*, *her* or *them* + *and* + noun phrase (or noun phrase + *and me*) as the subject, sometimes picked up again by *we*; also *him and I*, a me-first list, and an object pronoun as the subject of a gerund.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words for a line-initial *me and* + word (count 15); 0.01 per 1,000 for a line-initial *him*, *her* or *them* + *and* + subject (count 6); under 0.01 per 1,000 for the dash-led version (count 2). These catch only the sentence-initial cases. Episodes: 60 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and Christopher most, then Junior, Janice, Bobby, Artie, Paulie, Adriana and A.J.; agents and second-language speakers too. One speaker uses both this and the standard form in the same story.
- **What it does:** names who did something together: shared memories, old feuds, a joint plan or partnership, a trip, a report that a job is done, a heated denial of who was present. With a gerund it comments on a shared meal or a pictured arrangement.
- **Example:** *Me and Dana fixed the pipeline last night.*

### Stutter restart

- **Pattern:** a word or first sound broken off and started again: short fragment + hyphen + the same word.
- **How often:** rate 0.23 per 1,000 words (count 100). Not logged as a construction in the season notes; the character notes record stuttered pronoun restarts in Tony's speech under strain.
- **Who:** the statistics cannot say; the notes name Tony when pressed.
- **What it does:** marks a speaker caught off guard, searching for footing or about to say something hard.
- **Example:** *I, I didn't, w-, we never touched that table.*

### Verbless fragments as full turns

- **Pattern:** a bare noun phrase, adjective, participle, number, place name or comma list standing alone as the whole utterance, often several in a row; *no* + noun with no *there is*; amount + *to* + person for a split; *and* + pronoun + predicate with no verb; strings of the shape *not a* + noun, *not a* + noun.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 58 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Paulie, Christopher and Carmela most, then Junior, Meadow, Artie, Silvio, Janice and Johnny Sack; a nurse and a croupier too.
- **What it does:** the workhorse of quick exchanges: one-word answers, verdicts on people and places, prices, deadlines, diagnoses, car specs, injury reports, inventories, epitaphs and curses at weather, noise or food. Johnny Sack sets meetings as bare lists of day, place and time; a chain of noun phrases can set a scene before a punch line or tell a whole life story.
- **Example:** *Tuesday. Nine sharp. Your office, not mine.*

### Singular verb with a plural subject

- **Pattern:** *there's*, *here's*, *where's*, *how's* or *what's* + plural noun phrase; plural subject, *you*, *we* or *they* + *was*; *does* + plural subject; *this* or *that* + a plural sum treated as one item; *you's* for *you are*; once the reverse, a plural verb after *the* + singular head + *of* + plural noun.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words for *there's* + a number or common plural (count 9); 0.02 per 1,000 for *you*, *we* or *they* + *was* (count 7). Both catch a small slice of the cases. Episodes: 57 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Carmela, Janice and Paulie; it cuts across education levels, reaching a hotel clerk, a lawyer, a scientist mid-lecture, school staff and once the therapist. Ralph slides into *we was* in a story about his youth while using *were* in the same story.
- **What it does:** ordinary casual agreement in counts, invitations, reports, boasts and war stories, usually when a list or a name comes between subject and verb.
- **Example:** *There's three tickets still open from last sprint.*

### Money talk with the units left out

- **Pattern:** bare number with *thousand* or *dollars* understood; number + *grand*, *bucks*, *k*, *G's* or *mil*; *a million* + bare hundreds digit; *points* for a percentage; *tops* for an upper limit.
- **How often:** rate 0.21 per 1,000 words (count 92). Episodes: 4 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Johnny Sack, Vito, Silvio and Feech; poker players and dealers.
- **What it does:** the default way to name loot, loans and their weekly payment, budgets, grosses, settlements, bets, prices and trade-in values, with the thousands left implied. A plural *grands* and a dollar sign doubled with *bucks* each appear once and may be subtitle slips.
- **Example:** *The license is forty a year, sixty tops.*

### Don't with a third-person singular subject

- **Pattern:** *he*, *she*, *it*, *that* or a singular noun + *don't* + bare verb; also a tag *don't it?*
- **How often:** rate 0.16 per 1,000 words for *he*, *she*, *it*, *that* or *this* + *don't* (count 70). Episodes: 53 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher and Paulie most, then Adriana, Bobby, Junior, Feech and Richie; older men, the crew and second-language speakers; in one episode only an old gangster film on television. A man talking with Junior picks it up.
- **What it does:** flat pronouncements, gossip about how someone behaves, quick judgments, curt dismissals, maxims and business verdicts, often at the speaker's most emphatic. Nobody marks or corrects it, and the same speakers use *doesn't* elsewhere, so it marks heat and plain speaking.
- **Example:** *It don't matter which branch, the build fails anyway.*

### Historical present in stories

- **Pattern:** past events told with present-tense verbs, often after a past-tense setup or a time phrase and turned on with *so*, *and* or *suddenly*; *says*, *goes* or *comes* as reporting verbs; a switch back to the past for the last action.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 51 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher and Junior most, then Carmela, Paulie, Ralph, Janice, Feech and Little Carmine; Paulie and Janice slide into it whenever a story gets good.
- **What it does:** puts the listener inside the scene: anecdotes, jokes, prison stories, dreams, accidents, grievances and gossip chains. *So* often marks the turn to the climax, and speakers often switch tense mid-story. Tony uses it to replay how bad news reached him; a long prison stretch can be compressed into one present-tense sentence.
- **Example:** *So I'm in the standup, and the manager walks in, and he says the launch is off.*

### Like as a conjunction

- **Pattern:** *like* + full clause meaning *as*, *as if* or *the way*; *look*, *act*, *feel*, *sound* or *pretend* + *like* + clause; *like* + person + *says* introducing a saying; *it's not like* + clause.
- **How often:** rate 0.17 per 1,000 words after verbs of looking, acting, feeling, sounding, talking, treating and pretending (count 76). Episodes: 22 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and A.J. most, then Christopher, Johnny Sack, Richie, Janice, Bobby and the therapist.
- **What it does:** introduces a comparison, a quoted authority or a crude saying; describes how someone acts or pretends; mocks a claim or throws an argument back. Carmela uses it with an edge when handing over terms in writing, and A.J. argues that a refusal is unjustified with the *it's not like* frame.
- **Example:** *He acts like the outage was my idea.*

### Article dropped before a familiar noun

- **Pattern:** a singular count noun, kin term, role noun or epithet with no *a* or *the*, usually at the start of a clause: *guy*, *thing*, *kid*, *doctor*, *problem*, *reason*, *truth*; also clipped superlatives and ordinals, *go to* + bare place noun, and greetings that lose *good*.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 48 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Junior, Carmela, Adriana, Meadow, Bobby and Silvio. Readers note that some cases may be subtitle compression, and that the native pattern stays at the start of the clause, unlike the article slips of second-language speakers.
- **What it does:** gives reports, time estimates and joke setups a clipped, headline feel; opens anecdotes; turns a person into a type, sometimes with an insulting epithet in the subject slot; keeps a victim nameless; and clips a framing noun before the speaker pivots to the real point.
- **Example:** *Guy from the bank called. Problem is, nobody signed the form.*

### Do-support with got

- **Pattern:** (wh-word) + *do*, *does* or *don't* + subject + *got* (+ noun phrase or *to* + verb); subject + *don't got*; the same with *gotta*, as in *don't gotta* + verb.
- **How often:** rate 0.16 per 1,000 words (count 70). Episodes: 36 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Vito, Paulie, Bobby and Junior; agents use it when they pick up street register, and a butcher uses it to admit he cannot pay.
- **What it does:** treats *got* as an ordinary main verb that takes *do*. It opens collections, debriefings and calls by asking what someone has brought, brushes off hospitality or bystanders, makes a pressure question that expects a quick no, and gives a courteous release from an obligation.
- **Example:** *Do you got the logs, or don't you?*

### Comprehension checks

- **Pattern:** order or statement + *understand?*, *hear me?*, *got it?* or *we clear?*
- **How often:** rate 0.16 per 1,000 words (count 70). Episodes: 1 in the notes, which list it as a construction only inside the first season's entry on tags added to orders; the second-, third- and fourth-season notes log it as a discourse move instead.
- **Who:** Tony above all. The character notes show him making men say aloud that they understand and sealing threats with an Italian check of understanding; the second-season notes name Silvio as the likely speaker of a repeated check, and the first-season notes find the tag on orders from Christopher and the therapist too.
- **What it does:** demands acknowledgment and makes compliance explicit; it turns an order or a threat into something the listener has to agree to out loud.
- **Example:** *Nobody touches the billing code, understand?*

### Oath tags

- **Pattern:** clause + *goddamn it*, *for Christ's sake* or a similar oath at the end, sometimes repeated; or a sworn oath standing alone.
- **How often:** rate 0.15 per 1,000 words (count 65). Episodes: 2 in the notes.
- **Who:** Junior, Ralph, Adriana, Johnny Sack and Tony.
- **What it does:** caps an outburst: urging a horse on, fearing a missed news segment, grudging agreement to an errand, a courtroom demand, a denial of an affair.
- **Example:** *Restart the service, for Christ's sake.*

### Adjective form as an adverb

- **Pattern:** verb + *good*, *bad*, *nice*, *quick*, *easy*, *real*, *direct*, *different*, *safe* or *personal* where written English has the -*ly* form; *real* + adjective for *really*; *big* after a phrase meaning heavily.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words for *good* or *bad* after a verb (count 25); 0.05 per 1,000 for *real* + adjective (count 23). Episodes: 45 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Junior, A.J., Carmela, Ralph and Paulie; a polished executive and an FBI agent too.
- **What it does:** ordinary talk about performance, results, moods, sleep, how a marriage or a school year is going, and warnings dressed as polite requests. *Real* works as a general intensifier in instructions and boasts.
- **Example:** *The demo went real good, they took it personal when it crashed.*

### Better without had

- **Pattern:** (subject) + *better* (+ *not*) + bare verb, with *had* left out; *better* alone as a clipped warning; once *should better*.
- **How often:** rate 0.14 per 1,000 words (count 60). Episodes: 18 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Meadow, Janice, Paulie and Carmela.
- **What it does:** delivers advice with an edge or an outright warning, from a domestic nudge to a threat with menace in it; also mock demands, half-joking expectations and an emphatic agreement formula.
- **Example:** *You better not merge that before I'm back.*

### Spelled back-channels

- **Pattern:** spelled assent tokens *mm-hmm* and *uh-huh*; dissent tokens *uh-uh* and *mm-mm*; the warning interjection *uh-oh*.
- **How often:** assent 0.14 per 1,000 words (count 60); dissent 0.02 per 1,000 (count 9); *uh-oh* 0.01 per 1,000 (count 6). Not logged as constructions in the season notes, though their spelling lists record these tokens and the third-season notes log a doubled back-channel as a move.
- **Who:** the statistics cannot say.
- **What it does:** keeps the other speaker going, answers a yes/no question without a word, or flags trouble just seen.
- **Example:** *Uh-huh. Uh-huh. And then the cache filled up?*

### Closing all right? and okay? on an order

- **Pattern:** order or statement + *all right?* or *okay?* as a closing tag.
- **How often:** rate 0.13 per 1,000 words for a comma + *all right?* (count 59). Episodes: 3 in the notes, which mention it inside broader entries on tags.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, Christopher and Junior.
- **What it does:** rounds off an order without inviting refusal, turns it into a checked agreement, and closes an angry statement.
- **Example:** *Ship the hotfix tonight, all right?*

### Declarative questions

- **Pattern:** statement word order with a rising tone and no inversion, often negative, often after *So* or *Now*, sometimes carrying *still* or a stressed *should*; sometimes a bare noun phrase with a rising tone.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 42 in the notes.
- **Who:** Carmela and Tony most, then Junior, Paulie, Meadow, Christopher, A.J. and Adriana.
- **What it does:** more often signals surprise, disbelief, suspicion or a veiled threat than a request for information. It checks a half-expected fact and accuses in the same breath, since the question already contains the charge. A *so*-prefaced version tests whether an absurd conclusion holds, and Tony uses it again and again in therapy. Gossip is built from chains of them, each detail offered for a nod, and in marital fights they carry outrage.
- **Example:** *So you rebooted the server during the demo?*

### I'm just saying

- **Pattern:** *I'm saying* or *I'm just saying* (also spelled *sayin'*) tacked after a pointed remark; *all's I'm saying* and clause + *is all* + *I'm saying* are related closers.
- **How often:** rate 0.13 per 1,000 words (count 56), plus 0.03 per 1,000 for the *sayin'* spelling (count 13). The tag itself is not logged as a construction in the season notes; the related *is all*, *is what* and *all's* closers turn up inside other entries in three seasons (episodes: 3 in the notes), and the character notes record Paulie needling someone and then retreating into a just-saying hedge.
- **Who:** Paulie in the notes; the statistics cannot say more.
- **What it does:** delivers a jab and then disowns its force, leaving the point on the table while claiming no offense was meant.
- **Example:** *The tests are flaky. I'm just saying.*

### Expletive infixation

- **Pattern:** first part of a word, name, number, clock time or set phrase + *fucking* (or *freaking*, *frigging*) + the rest.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words (count 28). Episodes: 40 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Paulie, Silvio, Junior and Phil; a teenager's infix greets good news and earns a swear fine.
- **What it does:** kept for peak moments: stunned disbelief, exasperated relief, maximum sarcasm, mock awe, fake enthusiasm about a gift. It splits place names, brand names, a rival's name, a letter grade, the time of a late-night call, oaths and holy names, and mocks a friend's flowery phrasing.
- **Example:** *Un-fucking-believable, the backup folder is empty.*

### Like as a hedge and approximator

- **Pattern:** *like* inserted before a number, time span, example, reaction, name or verb phrase; *for, like,* + duration.
- **How often:** not measured as a hedge by the statistics. Episodes: 40 in the notes.
- **Who:** the teenagers above all, A.J. and Meadow, and Adriana; Christopher, Carmela and Tony now and then. Older men use it mostly before numbers, and a military interviewer seizes on it and mocks it.
- **What it does:** hedges sums, ages, prices and times in stories, softens suggestions, refusals and verdicts, and stalls while reaching for a name. Older speakers also use it sarcastically, to shrink how long someone stayed.
- **Example:** *It took, like, forty minutes to compile.*

### Was in if and wish clauses

- **Pattern:** *if* or *wish* + subject + *was* (or *wasn't*, *was to*) where written English has *were*; a compressed opener built on *if I was* + *you*.
- **How often:** rate 0.12 per 1,000 words (count 53). Episodes: 28 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Carmela, Junior and Bobby; Livia scolding a grandchild, and the therapist once.
- **What it does:** the ordinary spoken counterfactual. Readers note that the standard *were* is hardly heard in these slots, except from a speaker in a loftier mode. It carries credit claims, moral hypotheticals, insulting or wistful wishes, a widow's grief, a veiled warning, and with *was to* it frames a sensitive offer as a hypothetical.
- **Example:** *If I was you, I'd pin that dependency.*

### Perfect without have

- **Pattern:** subject + *been*, *seen*, *gotten*, *ever* or *never* + participle, -ing form, adjective or place, with *have* or *has* left out; sometimes the subject goes too.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 38 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Junior, Paulie, Pussy, Carmela and Artie; a surgeon too.
- **What it does:** marks time running up to now, usually with irritation: how long someone has waited, where someone has been, how long a suspicion or a betrayal has gone on. It backs claims to rights from years served, greets someone back after a long absence, and with the subject gone it opens a confession.
- **Example:** *I been telling you about that memory leak since March.*

### Imperative with an overt you

- **Pattern:** (name,) *you* + bare verb as an order, often chained (*you* do this, *you* do that); *don't you* (+ *ever*, *dare* or an expletive) + verb; *you* + present tense for a standing rule; *you will* restating an order; *never you mind*; *you,* + order singling out each listener in turn.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words for *don't you ever* and similar (count 11); 0.01 per 1,000 for *never you mind* (count 4). Episodes: 34 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony's signature way of giving orders; Carmela, Junior and Christopher, and parents speaking to children; a surgeon handing off work. Junior uses the old *never you mind* to shut down prying, and second-language speakers shout it under attack.
- **What it does:** makes an order pointed and personal, places the responsibility squarely on the listener, assigns tasks step by step, and hands out punishments to each child in turn. At its angriest it becomes a warning; a trailing *now* can urge or soften, and from a host it can add warmth.
- **Example:** *You write the migration, you test it, you own it.*

### Listen to me

- **Pattern:** *listen to me* as a frame before the point, usually opening the turn.
- **How often:** rate 0.10 per 1,000 words (count 42). Not logged as a construction in the season notes; the character notes record Tony claiming the floor with *look*, *listen* or a name, Carmela opening with *listen* and a relative's name, and Christopher opening with *listen* and an initial.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and Christopher in the character notes.
- **What it does:** claims the floor before a demand, a warning or a serious point, and marks that what follows is not up for debate.
- **Example:** *Listen to me, nobody deploys on a Friday.*

### Fronting for focus

- **Pattern:** object, amount, time span, count, predicate or prepositional phrase first + subject + verb, with no pronoun copy; a bare time or measure phrase up front with no *for* or *in*; *such* + noun phrase fronted; a noun phrase fronted before *imagine* + a *when*-clause; the offense named first, then a courtly apology formula; once, question word order used as an exclamation.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 33 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Carmela, Christopher, Paulie, Junior, Ralph and Johnny Sack, who states claims as logical conclusions with the object fronted; Carmela's friends gossiping, and Artie apologizing for a slow kitchen.
- **What it does:** puts the outrageous figure, the price, the wasted time, the grievance or the claimed thing first, so the listener hears the point before the rest of the clause; also contrast, as in the one thing someone can tolerate. A newscaster leads a story with a formal version, and a fronted duration gives a dream accusation a stiff, foreign feel.
- **Example:** *Three weeks I waited for that code review.*

### 'll and 'd on a noun or name

- **Pattern:** noun or name + contracted *'ll* or *'d*.
- **How often:** rate 0.09 per 1,000 words (count 40). Not logged as a construction in the season notes, though their spelling lists record names and nouns with *'ll* or *'d* attached.
- **Who:** the statistics cannot say.
- **What it does:** carries the reduced future or conditional straight onto a full noun subject, the way pronouns take it, in quick predictions and promises.
- **Example:** *My manager'll want the numbers by noon.*

### You know as a filler

- **Pattern:** *you know* at a clause edge, mid-clause or just before a hard-to-find word; *you know* + what-clause as a bid for agreement; *you know what* + a first-person clause as a check.
- **How often:** rate 0.09 per 1,000 words for *you know what* followed by *I* (count 38); *you know* itself is the second most frequent two-word sequence in the corpus (count 1,257). Episodes: 5 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Adriana and Carmela; a young associate asking his boss for protection; a visiting filmmaker giving notes.
- **What it does:** buys time before an awkward request or admission, hedges plans and confessions, invites sympathy, repairs a statement mid-stream, or hands the listener the job of finishing the thought.
- **Example:** *I was going to, you know, refactor it first.*

### Or what? tag

- **Pattern:** yes/no question or statement + *or what?*; *Is that* + evaluative phrase + *or what?*
- **How often:** rate 0.08 per 1,000 words (count 37). Episodes: 9 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Paulie, Meadow, Artie, Junior, Richie and Silvio.
- **What it does:** adds impatience, contempt or teasing pressure and pushes for a decision; turns a frustrated question into an exclamation; dresses admiration as a question, as when Artie demands praise for his fish; covers a name the speaker cannot recall.
- **Example:** *Are we shipping this today or what?*

### All as an intensifier

- **Pattern:** *be*, *get* or *got* (+ someone) + *all* + adjective or participle; also *major* after or before a noun and *super* + adjective.
- **How often:** rate 0.08 per 1,000 words for *all* + adjective after *be* or *get* (count 37). Episodes: 8 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher and Meadow; *major* is Christopher's hype word, and *super* belongs to children and club-goers.
- **What it does:** describes someone getting upset, excited or worked up; Tony mocks Carmela's new tolerance with it and contrasts his own calm with a seasick companion's.
- **Example:** *He got all worked up about the font.*

### What ... for meaning why

- **Pattern:** *what* + helping verb + subject + verb ... + *for?*, or a bare *what for?*
- **How often:** rate 0.08 per 1,000 words (count 34). Episodes: 8 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Bobby, Ralph, Junior and Phil.
- **What it does:** demands a justification rather than information, with irritation or disbelief: about a pointless call, a disapproving look, harm done to a person or an animal. It also sets up a bar joke and opens one of Tony's rhetorical history lessons.
- **Example:** *What'd you rename the folder for?*

### Assurance frames: tell you, believe me

- **Pattern:** *let me* + *tell you*, *I'll tell you*, *I tell you*, *I'm telling you* or *believe me*, opening or closing a clause or inserted mid-sentence.
- **How often:** rate 0.08 per 1,000 words for *let me* + *tell you* (count 34). Episodes: 4 in the notes.
- **Who:** Christopher and Tony, then Janice, Carmela, Silvio and Vito.
- **What it does:** vouches for the speaker's own claim and marks candor or emphasis before a complaint, an admission, a boast or a promise.
- **Example:** *Let me tell you, that vendor never ships on time.*

### What are you, X?

- **Pattern:** *What* + *be* + subject, + guessed noun phrase or adjective?; first-person *what am I* + *a* + noun?; *What is this,* + absurd label?
- **How often:** rate 0.07 per 1,000 words with a second- or third-person subject (count 29); 0.02 per 1,000 for the first-person version (count 9). Episodes: 27 in the notes.
- **Who:** Christopher and Tony most, then Junior, Paulie, Bobby, Artie, Ralph, A.J. and Meadow.
- **What it does:** almost always a challenge: it proposes an absurd identity for the listener, the situation or the speaker, to mock a complaint, refuse a demand or reject being taken for a fool. Now and then it is a half-teasing question that offers its own answer.
- **Example:** *What are you, my code reviewer now?*

### Indefinite this

- **Pattern:** *this* or *these* + (adjective) + noun introducing a person or thing the listener has not heard of yet, in place of *a* or *some*; often after *there's*, *I got* or *so*.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words for *this* + a person noun after a story frame such as *there's* or *I know* (count 21). Episodes: 25 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Meadow, Paulie, Carmela, Janice, Junior and A.J.
- **What it does:** the default way to bring new people, places, schemes and objects into anecdotes, gossip, pitches and complaints, often with a tinge of contempt or vagueness. It can belittle something new, and it marks a newcomer who is already significant.
- **Example:** *So there's this intern who force-pushes to main.*

### Dropped prepositions

- **Pattern:** *go down the* + place or *over* + someone's house with *to* or *at* left out; *get out the* + noun with *of* gone; time phrases and holiday names with no *in*, *on* or *by*; *talk* + topic or amount with no *about*; a route with *from* left out; *tops out* + number; *deprive* + person + thing with no *of*.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words for *down the* + place after a motion verb (count 10); under 0.01 per 1,000 for *over* + someone's house (count 2). Episodes: 24 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and Christopher most, then Junior, Paulie, Richie and Bobby.
- **What it does:** regional place talk above all: the shore towns, visits and deliveries to someone's home, errands to familiar local places. It also clips shouted commands, a lawyer's phone shorthand, dates and payments due. The shore is the fixed case.
- **Example:** *We're going down the shore after the release.*

### Learner English: verb forms and agreement

- **Pattern:** bare verb where past tense, future or third-person -*s* is needed; simple present for a finished event or a planned future; present + *since* for a duration; *I* + *be* + place for a plan; *not gonna* with no *be*; *is* + participle for *has*; plural noun without -*s* after a number; plural verb with a singular collective; *may to* + verb.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 24 in the notes.
- **Who:** Furio, Svetlana, Irina, Annalisa and the other second-language speakers: Italian visitors, a Polish couple, Russian, French and other caregivers, Kim, shopkeepers and a deli owner.
- **What it does:** the most consistent mark of the second-language speakers, in accounts of what was done, offers about to be carried out, refusals on price and protests. Time is carried by adverbs or context. Tony once mimics the dropped -*s* in a mock-dim question.
- **Example:** *Yesterday I fix the router, tomorrow I call the company.*

### Will you as a tag or an exasperated order

- **Pattern:** order + *will you?*, *will ya?* or *would you?*; or *Will you* (+ *just* or *please*) + verb phrase? used as a command.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words for a comma + *will*, *would*, *can* or *could* + *you* or *ya?* (count 28). Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Carmela most, then Janice, Little Carmine, Artie and the therapist.
- **What it does:** softens a household request or an order to a sibling, uncle or driver, or shows impatience with a spouse. With a verb of looking it mocks someone's fears. From an educated man, the clipped *would ya* marks a slip into street register.
- **Example:** *Close the laptop for five minutes, will you?*

### Nonstandard past and participle forms

- **Pattern:** *seen*, *done*, *come*, *run*, *brung*, *snuck*, *shrunk* or *bust* as the simple past; *forbid* for *forbade*; *hung* for *hanged*; an unmarked -*t* past; *got* as the participle after *would've*; a simple past form in the participle slot.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words for *I*, *he*, *she*, *we* or *they* + *seen* or *done* + object (count 7). Episodes: 23 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Paulie, Bobby and Livia; a rapper in an otherwise polished persona; a doctor checking a treatment history.
- **What it does:** scattered through casual speech, often in charged moments: a boast about a sighting, a profit lament, a bitter reply, a bedside report, a hedge to an agent. The same speakers use the standard forms elsewhere, so it varies.
- **Example:** *I seen the error, so I done a rollback.*

### Remember? as a tag

- **Pattern:** statement or fragment + *, remember?*
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words (count 27). Episodes: 2 in the notes, which mention it inside broader tag entries.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Carmela and Paulie.
- **What it does:** checks shared knowledge and pins the listener to a past fact, often as half a demand or a reproach.
- **Example:** *You said Friday was fine, remember?*

### Sarcastic like-clause

- **Pattern:** *Like* + full clause, said as its own sentence or retort and meaning *as if*; often with *would ever*, *even* or a closing *please*.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words (count 26). Episodes: 17 in the notes.
- **Who:** the young speakers above all, Meadow, A.J. and Adriana, then Christopher and Carmela; Tony and Richie now and then.
- **What it does:** denies the other person's claim without arguing it: a boast called false, a fear mocked as absurd, news dismissed as obvious, a parent's point brushed off, an accusation of hypocrisy thrown back.
- **Example:** *Like he's ever read a changelog.*

### How come with statement order

- **Pattern:** *how come* + subject + verb with no inversion, or *how come* + noun phrase.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words (count 26). Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Christopher, Meadow, A.J. and Tony; Junior in his last years; the therapist once.
- **What it does:** a challenge-why: grievance at being passed over, a teenager arguing for privileges, a complaint dressed as a question about cause. It also serves as an innocent, nosy or probing why.
- **Example:** *How come nobody told me the API changed?*

### What's with + noun phrase

- **Pattern:** *what's*, *what is* or *what was* (+ *the fuck*) + *with* + noun phrase or person?
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words (count 26). Episodes: 10 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and Christopher; Adriana calling out prejudice; Tony B; a father before a threat.
- **What it does:** demands the reason for someone's behavior, mood, look or attitude, always with irritation, suspicion or mild reproach.
- **Example:** *What's with all the tabs open?*

### Ass for the person

- **Pattern:** possessive + (adjective) + *ass* as a stand-in for the whole person, as object or location; verb + possessive + *ass off*; *my ass* as an emphatic *me*.
- **How often:** rate 0.06 per 1,000 words after verbs such as *get*, *move*, *haul* or *save* (count 26). Episodes: 5 in the notes.
- **Who:** Richie, Ralph, Tony B, Paulie, Pussy and the crew.
- **What it does:** delivers someone in person, demands they show up, condemns saving oneself at others' cost, and stresses how hard someone works or how much they earn.
- **Example:** *Get your ass to the standup, it started ten minutes ago.*

### Learner English: articles

- **Pattern:** a noun phrase with no *a* or *the* where English needs one, or *the* added before a surname, a channel, a show, a drink, an activity or a course name; a numeral + singular noun; a bare compound in place of a possessive.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 23 in the notes.
- **Who:** Furio, Svetlana, Irina, Kim and the other second-language speakers: Russian, Polish, Czech, Italian and Spanish speakers, a nurse, a maid, a truck driver, imported gunmen. Tony echoes an Italian student's usage when retelling her talk.
- **What it does:** runs through nearly every sentence of some second-language speakers and marks them off from the native speakers, whose own article drop stays at the start of the clause.
- **Example:** *I make backup on server, then I watch the Netflix.*

### Relative clause with no relative pronoun

- **Pattern:** noun phrase + finite verb phrase describing it with no *who* or *that*, even in subject position, often after a cleft (*it was* X, *there's* X); once with *have* dropped too.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 22 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most; Johnny Sack, Artie, Carmela and the crew; a learner's string of descriptive clauses.
- **What it does:** introduces a person by what they do, credits or pins an act on someone, shifts blame inside a cleft, and packs excuses, pitches and hearsay into one quick clause.
- **Example:** *I know a guy fixes laptops for cash.*

### Clipped names and initials in address

- **Pattern:** a name cut after its first syllable (written with an apostrophe), reduced to a single initial, given a -*y* diminutive or a sing-song extension; initials standing for a person.
- **How often:** not measured as a construction; the clipped form *ton'* alone runs at 0.56 per 1,000 words (count 243) and *carm* at 0.24 (count 104). Episodes: 21 in the notes.
- **Who:** spouses, siblings, in-laws and the crew: Tony, Paulie, Carmela, Janice, Christopher, Bobby. Men under the boss address him by one syllable or one initial.
- **What it does:** marks intimacy and familiarity in banter and in tense moments alike; the boss is almost never called by his full first name on the phone, and the full form returns when an argument turns hostile or a speaker wants distance. A federal agent's clipped form of an informant's name is pointed familiarity, and Tony clips his mistress's name when he softens before bad news.
- **Example:** *Rob', you got a sec? D, you too.*

### Present tense for a state lasting up to now

- **Pattern:** time span + subject + present tense; present or present progressive + *for* or *since* + time span, or + *lately*, where written English uses the present perfect; *since* + subject + *is dead* for *since he died*.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 21 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Adriana and Johnny Sack; Pussy, Paulie, Junior, Hesh, Janice, Phil and Little Carmine; a restaurant host.
- **What it does:** dates a grievance, a fear or a routine from an event and vouches for a long acquaintance: how long someone has been dead, tailed or loyal, a dragging engagement, a repeated attempt to get through. Readers hear it as Italian-inflected or learner-like in some mouths.
- **Example:** *I work on this module since January.*

### Bare fuck as the question word

- **Pattern:** (*the*) *fuck* + (helper) + subject + verb, with *what* or *why* and often the helper dropped; standing alone with a question mark; *the fuck* + subject + helper! as a flat denial; a contracted *fuck's* for *what is*.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words (count 24). Episodes: 19 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most, then Paulie, Johnny Sack, Junior and Ralph.
- **What it does:** a fast, harsh opener for startled or hostile questions on arriving at a scene, at a visitor, at intruders, at someone talking too loudly about secret business; between men it can double as a rough greeting. Johnny Sack uses it as a flat contradiction and Tony as an emphatic denial.
- **Example:** *The fuck you doing in my terminal?*

### 's for does in questions

- **Pattern:** wh-word + *'s* (for *does*) + subject + bare verb; *how's* + subject + bare verb; *what good's* + subject + verb; the reduced *does* can stack with a second *'s* for *is*.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words (count 24). Episodes: 14 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Paulie, Artie and A.J.
- **What it does:** asks what someone wants, does, thinks or owns, often skeptically or to needle; asks the meaning of something unfamiliar; sneers about who really pays; once a polite follow-up about a peer's wife.
- **Example:** *What's he want with admin rights?*

### Complaint frames with with

- **Pattern:** (*enough*, *stop*, *always*, *again*, *still*, *come on*, *please*, *start*) + *with the* (or *this*, *that*) + noun phrase, usually with no main verb; *again with the* + noun?; *with the* + noun + *again*; order + *with that*; *already* added for impatience; a bare *start* heading off a complaint.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words for *enough*, *stop* and similar + *with the* (count 23); 0.01 per 1,000 for *again with the* (count 6). Episodes: 19 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Carmela most, then Christopher, the therapist, Johnny Sack, Phil and Meadow; Livia in the first season.
- **What it does:** a near-verbless jab at someone's recurring topic or habit (horror stories, nostalgia, therapy talk, pessimism, whistling, secrecy), labeling it tiresome and sidestepping the argument. In one therapist's mouth readers hear a Yiddish flavor, and a reply can echo the frame as agreement.
- **Example:** *Again with the tabs versus spaces?*

### Shit as a negative meaning anything

- **Pattern:** negated verb + *shit*, *fuck*, *dick* or *squat* in the slot of *anything*; *jack shit*.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words (count 23). Episodes: 7 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher, then Paulie; a beaten dealer denying any provocation.
- **What it does:** a stronger *nothing*: it seals a version of events, refuses a debt, admits nothing is known yet, or rules something out entirely.
- **Example:** *The logs don't tell us shit.*

### Try and + verb

- **Pattern:** *try and* + bare verb for *try to*; *be sure and* + verb for *be sure to*.
- **How often:** rate 0.05 per 1,000 words (count 22). Episodes: 3 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Silvio and A.J.; a surgeon calming a patient.
- **What it does:** softens bad news, a suggestion or a reassurance; Tony uses *be sure and* in a put-on polite phone voice.
- **Example:** *Try and get some sleep before the cutover.*

### Quotatives: goes, like, all, says

- **Pattern:** subject + *goes* or *go* + quoted speech; *be like* + quoted or imagined words; *be all* + quotation or state; *I says* in past-time stories; a doubled *said*; a trailing *I said* or *he says* after the quote.
- **How often:** rate 0.01 per 1,000 words for *be like* (count 4); under 0.01 per 1,000 each for *I says* (count 1) and narrative *goes* (count 1). Episodes: 20 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, A.J., Christopher, Ralph, Paulie, Meadow, Junior, Feech and Johnny Sack.
- **What it does:** introduces reported or acted-out speech in jokes, retellings, toasts and relayed messages. *Be like* stages a mocking scene; *be all* mimics a parent's nagging; the doubled verb sets up a punch line or relives an argument; a trailing *he says* reports a claim skeptically.
- **Example:** *So he goes, it works on my machine, and I'm like, great.*

### Copula left out of statements

- **Pattern:** subject + adjective, noun phrase, place, participle, -*ing* form or *gonna*, with *is*, *are* or *am* left out; *still* + subject + place with no *is*.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 18 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and others in quick asides; clustered in street registers: dealers, a rapper and his circle, hired shooters, robbers, young associates styling themselves on hip-hop, and second-language speakers. Readers tie the rapper's use to African American English and allow that a subtitle may sometimes drop the word.
- **What it does:** gives a rough, offhand sound to status reports, sizing-up, hostile verdicts and imagined speech.
- **Example:** *Server down again, everybody waiting on you.*

### Italian words clipped at the end

- **Pattern:** an Italian, Sicilian or dialect word or name cut before its final vowel or syllable (written with an apostrophe), set in an English sentence slot.
- **How often:** not measured as a construction; clipped forms such as *madonn'* (count 26) show up in the spelling counts. Episodes: 17 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Artie, Paulie, Carmela and Christopher; Janice, Bobby and the older relatives.
- **What it does:** marks the Italian layer and familiarity: food and wine names at family meals, exclamations of shock or admiration, toasts, pity words, insults, ethnic labels and family nicknames. The English grammar around the word stays intact.
- **Example:** *Grab me a biscott' and an espress', I'm stuck at my desk.*

### Wh-word left in place

- **Pattern:** the question word left in its ordinary slot at the end or middle of a clause (*for what?*, *to who?*, *how many times?*, noun phrase + *is what?*), or the other speaker's words repeated with *what* or *who* in place of the offending part; a questioning *what* standing where a number goes.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 17 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Johnny Sack, Carmela, Meadow and Paulie; Livia's tail *what?*; a detective and agents filling in a form.
- **What it does:** throws a claim back, protests a demand as baseless, demands a source, tallies how often something happened, makes the speaker hear an offensive word again, or estimates aloud and invites someone else to name the figure.
- **Example:** *You need the report by when?*

### Them as a demonstrative

- **Pattern:** *them* (or *'em*) + plural noun in place of *those*; in the first season also *they* + noun for *their*.
- **How often:** rate 0.04 per 1,000 words for *them* + a plural noun (count 19). Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** spread thin: Tony, Paulie or Christopher, Vito, Livia, a dealer, a crooked cop, an old coach, a church-hall seller.
- **What it does:** points at food, goods, merchandise or a kind of person already in play, in pep talks, orders at a diner and trade talk.
- **Example:** *Hand me them cables on the shelf.*

### The shit out of

- **Pattern:** verb + *the shit*, *the hell* or *the crap* + *out of* + object, meaning thoroughly; comparative + *than shit*; plural noun + *up the ass* for huge numbers.
- **How often:** rate 0.04 per 1,000 words (count 19). Episodes: 5 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Phil and Carmela; a carjacker and a schoolboy.
- **What it does:** strengthens violent verbs and strong feelings: retelling a father's beatings, a threat to hit someone, how much someone was missed, a catastrophizing count of legal troubles.
- **Example:** *That outage scared the hell out of the whole team.*

### All due respect, with no with

- **Pattern:** *all due respect* (no *with*) + vocative + challenge.
- **How often:** rate 0.04 per 1,000 words (count 19). Episodes: 1 in the notes.
- **Who:** Bobby, cutting off his boss in grief; the character notes add Silvio using it before and after each barb like a refrain, and Phil wrapping slights in respect formulas.
- **What it does:** a deference formula that licenses the disagreement that follows.
- **Example:** *All due respect, boss, that schema is a mess.*

### Some + noun as an exclamation

- **Pattern:** *some* + (adjective or expletive) + noun as an exclamation or verdict, often with *huh?*; *that was* or *must have been* + *some* + noun; *those were some* + plural noun.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words (count 8). Episodes: 16 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Paulie most; Christopher, Silvio, Johnny Sack, Janice, Livia and the therapist.
- **What it does:** mostly sarcastic, belittling a job, a vacation, a price, a holiday or someone's claim to a title; occasionally admiring, envious or outraged at someone's nerve.
- **Example:** *Some upgrade, huh? Nothing works.*

### Over by and out by for at

- **Pattern:** *over by*, *out by* or *by* + place for *at* or *near*; compound locatives such as *over at* or *up by* + place; *be over* + place with no *at*.
- **How often:** rate 0.04 per 1,000 words for *over by* or *out by* (count 17). Episodes: 7 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela and Christopher; Tony B and Kim.
- **What it does:** locates meetings, homes, shops and the spots inside a room, asks for a lift, and defends someone who says he was there.
- **Example:** *I'll be over by the coffee machine.*

### Kind of a and other kind-of hedges

- **Pattern:** *kind of a* or *some sort of* + *a* + noun; *this kind of* + adjective + noun; *kind of* or *kind of like* + adjective or noun phrase; *no kind of* + noun for an emphatic denial and *some kind of* + noun for scorn.
- **How often:** rate 0.04 per 1,000 words for *kind of a* or *sort of a* + noun (count 17). Episodes: 5 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Christopher, Janice, Tony B and A.J.; a visiting filmmaker.
- **What it does:** a vague lead-in when the speaker cannot or will not be precise; softens criticism; sneers at what a man is; denies something forcefully.
- **Example:** *It's kind of a hack, but it works.*

### Minced oaths and clipped taboo words

- **Pattern:** *friggin'*, *freakin'* or *frigging* in place of the strong expletive; the first letter + -*in'*; *the* + letter + -*word*; a single letter standing for a drug.
- **How often:** *freakin'* 0.04 per 1,000 words (count 19), *friggin'* 0.04 (count 16), *frigging* 0.01 (count 4); a clipped taboo word under 0.01 (count 1). Episodes: 4 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, who censors himself this way with his wife, his therapist and his children at the table and uses the full form with men; Carmela's swear-fine policy and A.J.'s report on his father; dealers naming a drug by its initial. The expletive notes add that milder forms appear with children and in polite company.
- **What it does:** lets a speaker curse without cursing hard, and marks who is in the room.
- **Example:** *This freakin' printer jammed again.*

### Would have in the if-clause and other counterfactual tenses

- **Pattern:** *if* + subject + *would have* or *'d have* + participle, + *would have* + participle; *wish* + subject + *would've* + participle; *if* + simple past where the past perfect is standard; *would have liked* + *to have* + participle; *if* + past + *may* for *might*.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words for *would have* inside an *if* or *wish* clause (count 7). Episodes: 15 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Junior, A.J., Christopher, Adriana, Bobby and Paulie; the therapist; an agent handling an informant.
- **What it does:** regrets, near misses, heated blame and scolding questions about disasters that almost happened, usually where the speaker is improvising under pressure.
- **Example:** *If the vendor would've called, we'd have patched it.*

### On account of

- **Pattern:** *on account of* + noun phrase, gerund or full clause, meaning *because* (*of*); also *'cause of* + noun phrase.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words (count 15). Episodes: 9 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then A.J.; Phil in the character notes.
- **What it does:** assigns blame and gives reasons with a slightly mock-formal ring, whether narrating the past, relaying a request or explaining a rule.
- **Example:** *We're late on account of the vendor never called back.*

### Nouns and adjectives turned into verbs

- **Pattern:** a noun, compound, adjective or proper name used with tense or an object; a famous name + -*ed* as a verb alluding to an incident; noun + -*ed out* meaning sated; compound -*ing* nouns for an occasion.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 13 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Christopher, then Meadow, Ralph, Vito and agents; a school official in jargon.
- **What it does:** supplies terse verbs for firing, beating, robbing, tipping someone off, killing, running a crew or idling in front of the television, and turns gossip or evasion into an action.
- **Example:** *He totally Slacked me at midnight, then ghosted the thread.*

### You + epithet as address

- **Pattern:** *you* + (stacked adjectives or expletives) + insult noun, as a term of address or tacked onto a command; *you* + epithet + *you*; *you* + family or group name; *all you* + plural noun.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 13 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Junior, Bobby, Artie and Paulie; the therapist hurls one at her own therapist.
- **What it does:** shames, attacks or affectionately abuses depending on the pair; stacked modifiers mark real fury, and the *you* ... *you* sandwich can be a warm greeting to an old friend.
- **Example:** *Get back to work, you lazy genius.*

### The -wise suffix

- **Pattern:** noun or noun phrase + -*wise*, meaning *as regards*, sometimes stacked and closed with *whatever*.
- **How often:** rate 0.02 per 1,000 words (count 10). Episodes: 12 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Carmela; a nurse, agents, a school counselor and an artist.
- **What it does:** sounds managerial, clinical or official: scores, grades, personnel, loan interest, sleep-test results. Tony also uses it jokingly and in a formal speech.
- **Example:** *Budget-wise we're fine, timeline-wise, not so much.*

### Foreign words in an English frame

- **Pattern:** an Italian, Sicilian, Yiddish, French or Spanish word, exclamation, toast or short phrase dropped into an English clause, sometimes glossed in English right after; once an English noun inside an Italian frame.
- **How often:** under the statistics only two fixed phrases were counted: an Italian curse and the word-search filler meaning how do you say, 0.01 per 1,000 words each (count 4 each). Episodes: 12 in the notes.
- **Who:** Artie and Tony most, then Paulie, Carmela, Junior and Furio; a priest and others borrowing Yiddish; French and French-Canadian speakers.
- **What it does:** greetings, endearments, apologies, toasts, mealtime calls, hushing, insults, oaths, blessings for the dead and acts the speaker will not name in English; Artie borrows French to sound worldly.
- **Example:** *Ecco, the build finally passed.*

### Demonstrative before a name or a disliked group

- **Pattern:** *this*, *that* or *these* + (expletive or adjective) + proper name, epithet or group noun; *this one* or *that one* + predicate; *that was a* + noun about a person; stacked as *this* + noun phrase, *this* + name.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 12 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Junior, Paulie and Ralph; Carmela, Hesh, Johnny Sack and the therapist now and then.
- **What it does:** distances the speaker or marks someone as a known nuisance: a rival, a long-dead traitor, a daughter's new friend, a teacher to blame. With a group noun it lumps outsiders, young men or a whole profession together for dismissal; occasionally it carries grudging admiration or a formal tribute.
- **Example:** *This Kevin from accounting, he wants another meeting.*

### Adverbs with an extra -s

- **Pattern:** *somewheres*, *nowheres*, *anywheres*, *elsewheres*; sentence-final *anyways*.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words (count 12). Episodes: 6 in the notes.
- **Who:** Christopher and Tony; once a relative advising a boy.
- **What it does:** turns up in complaints, orders, a screenplay pitch and inside a negative-concord order; *anyways* closes a joke or a challenge.
- **Example:** *Take the old monitors somewheres else, anyways.*

### Brush-off with a care question

- **Pattern:** *what* + *do* + subject + *care*, or a ruder idiom of caring in the same slot, meaning why should I care; sometimes blended with the negative swear idiom.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words (count 13). Episodes: 6 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most; Paulie, Christopher and Phil.
- **What it does:** brushes off a point, a charge or someone else's show of concern as none of the speaker's business.
- **Example:** *What do I care which framework they pick?*

### Reduced modal perfects: coulda, shoulda

- **Pattern:** *should*, *could*, *would* or *must* + *a* (the reduced *have*) + participle; *should* + participle with *have* dropped entirely.
- **How often:** rate 0.03 per 1,000 words for modal + *a* + participle (count 11); under 0.01 per 1,000 with *have* dropped (count 1). Episodes: 1 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and a parent.
- **What it does:** mild reproach about what someone ought to have done, and fantasies or panicked guesses about what could have happened.
- **Example:** *You coulda asked before you wiped the drive.*

### Lay for lie

- **Pattern:** *lay*, *laying* or *laid* used for intransitive *lie*, *lying* or *lay*, with no object; once an archaic *lay without*.
- **How often:** rate 0.01 per 1,000 words for *laying* or *lay* + *down*, *around*, *in bed* and similar (count 5). Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Carmela, Artie, Janice and Tony; an educated counselor on a late phone call; a priest in a churchly phrase.
- **What it does:** ordinary talk of lying in bed, sunbathing, moping at home or lying low, and an image of someone waiting patiently to strike.
- **Example:** *He's been laying on the couch since the layoffs.*

### Name + here as a pointer or label

- **Pattern:** name, famous name or label + *here*, *over here* or *there*, said of someone present or just mentioned; *this one here*; *that* + noun + *over there* for distance.

- **Who:** Tony and Christopher most; Silvio, Bobby, Janice and Junior; Phil with a sneer.
- **What it does:** presents a witness, points out a man while handing him something, offers a friend's services, or turns a label into a mocking nickname for someone in the room.
- **Example:** *Einstein here thinks the cache is optional.*

### No? as a tag

- **Pattern:** statement + *, no?*
- **How often:** not measured on its own; the statistics fold it into the tag-question count above. Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Artie and Paulie; Johnny Sack, Hesh, Richie and Meadow.
- **What it does:** turns a proposal or a rueful observation into a bid for agreement without stating a demand, softens a mild objection, caps a mocking hypothetical, and coaxes or teases. Readers hear it as Italian-flavored, as when wine is being served.
- **Example:** *We could just restart it, no?*

### Pseudo-clefts and doubled copulas

- **Pattern:** *what* + subject + verb (+ *is*) + verb phrase, noun phrase or clause, with the copula sometimes dropped or doubled; *the thing is* + clause; *you know who* + clause + *is* + noun phrase; *is that all* + clause + *is* + noun phrase?
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Adriana, Paulie, Bobby and Johnny Sack.
- **What it does:** introduces the key point of a deal, a pitch, a plan or a piece of advice; assigns someone a part in a scheme; mocks what a spouse supposedly wants; raises attraction indirectly; scolds.
- **Example:** *What you gotta do is, you gotta read the error first.*

### Third-person self-reference

- **Pattern:** the speaker names himself or herself by own name, a kin title (*Daddy*, *Grandma*, *Uncle* + name), a role or a mock title, or *a man* + verb, in place of *I* or *me*.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Junior and Livia most, then Carmela, Tony, Paulie, Janice, Bobby, Meadow, Hesh and Kim.
- **What it does:** claims respect or rank, invokes family tradition, casts the speaker as the wronged party or as an ordinary man being imposed upon, coaxes a child or a younger man, and mocks oneself. Kim's use makes him sound like a firm rather than a person.
- **Example:** *Daddy's fixing the wifi, Daddy needs five minutes.*

### Verbless orders and offers

- **Pattern:** a noun phrase (+ vocative, *please*, time or place) standing for a whole request, offer, order or appointment; *no* + noun as a rule; noun + *over*; a drink ordered by brand and preparation alone; a counter order as a noun phrase + *hold the* + ingredient; *little* + noun as an offer; a single particle as a command.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 11 in the notes.
- **Who:** Christopher, Tony and Paulie; Janice and Bobby as hosts; hospital staff in an emergency.
- **What it does:** hospitality and service, house rules, meeting arrangements, errands and urgent instructions in two or three words; the setting supplies the missing verb.
- **Example:** *Coffee? Little biscotti? Meeting, three o'clock, my office.*

### Verb phrases chained without and

- **Pattern:** verb phrase, verb phrase (, verb phrase) with the same subject and no *and*, *but* or *so*; a reason clause set beside its result with no *because*.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 10 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Carmela, Christopher, Richie and Junior; an FBI agent.
- **What it does:** lays out practical steps, travel instructions, a pitch, a cover story or a threat in one breath, and fires rapid orders in a crisis.
- **Example:** *Pull the branch, run the tests, ship it, go home.*

### Sentence-final here as a stance marker

- **Pattern:** clause + *here* (or *over here*, *now*), meaning *in this situation* rather than a place.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 10 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, Junior, Ralph and Christopher; a priest, the therapist and Richie.
- **What it does:** works like a verbal finger tap: presses the listener to notice the speaker's plight, insists on seriousness, pushes back on being misjudged, signals impatience, or adds an edge to sarcasm.
- **Example:** *I'm trying to debug here.*

### Orders stated as a settled future

- **Pattern:** *you're gonna* + verb, often in a series; *you will* or *you'll* + verb as a command; *nobody is* + -ing; a third-person present statement about the future; as a question, *you're gonna* + verb phrase? as a warning.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 10 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony above all; Johnny Sack; an escort and a sponsor; an accented dream figure.
- **What it does:** issues orders as predictions, so there is no room to refuse: a breakup narrated in advance, rehab terms, a ban on revenge, a rule of secrecy, a son told he will leave home. The question form warns the listener against going on.
- **Example:** *You're gonna write the postmortem, and you're gonna send it to everyone.*

### Thing as a vague label

- **Pattern:** *the*, *this* or *that* + (noun or modifier) + *thing* (or *business*), standing for an event, deal, illness, arrangement or the whole organization; *a* + adjective + *thing* classifying a phenomenon.
- **How often:** not measured by the statistics. Episodes: 10 in the notes.
- **Who:** Tony and Paulie most; Livia, Christopher and the crew.
- **What it does:** lets speakers avoid naming what is awkward or illegal (a killing, a possible tumor, a sexual side effect, a setup, the marriage trouble), partly as caution on monitored lines, and bundles a topic into one dismissible package.
- **Example:** *About that database thing last week, we good?*

### How about and other proposal frames

- **Pattern:** *how about* or *how's about* + subject + verb phrase, gerund or noun phrase; *how's* + place or time? to propose a meeting; *what d'you say* + (*we*) + bare clause with no *if* or *that*.

# Discourse

This file describes how a conversation moves in the dialect: how lines open, what fills and tags them, how a point gets closed, the conversational moves people make, how each kind of speech act is done, and which topics keep coming back. It was built from a full read of all 86 episodes, whose readers' notes supply the moves, the speakers and the episode counts, plus word counts over the whole corpus of 437,410 words in 52,258 subtitle lines, which supply every rate and share. It contains no quoted dialogue: the only words taken from the transcripts are single terms and expressions of three words or fewer, shown in quotation marks in form and said fields, and every example exchange is invented.

## Openers

Counts are of subtitle lines that begin with the form, out of 52,258 lines. A line is a subtitle, not a speaker's whole turn, so a long turn spans several lines and each of them can open with one of these forms. Every form was counted on its own, so a line that opens with a longer form is also counted under the shorter form it starts with, and the shares do not add up; together the 137 forms open at most one line in five. Sentences are short (median five words, 56% of them five words or fewer) and 23% of lines contain a question mark, which is why a bare opener so often carries the whole turn.

Measured closing constructions: a curse plus a name or pronoun at the start of a line, used to dismiss, 166 times (0.38); a complaint frame that opens with an order to stop with something, 23 times (0.05); an impatient trailing word for enough already, 7 times (0.02); and a shrug asking what the speaker could care, 13 times (0.03).

The readers logged these ways of ending a point, each covered as a move below:

- **Decree.** A fixed phrase declares the matter settled or names the bottom line, or a flat one-word assent ends the argument while signaling resentment (see Shutting a topic down).
- **Clincher.** A proverb, a parental saying or a general truth caps the point, sometimes cut to its first words (see Proverb or maxim as the last word).
- **Shrug.** A stock question about what anyone can do, or a line that life is like that, closes a loss or a grievance and lets the talk move on (see Fatalistic shrug).
- **Verdict.** A long story or an elder's war story ends on one word: a place name, an adjective, a moral (see Story as argument).
- **Seal.** An order ends with a comprehension check, and a rule with a demand that the listener say it back (see Comprehension check and agreement tag).
- **Curse.** An exchange ends with a curse aimed at the other person, or a person, a worry or a topic is written off with one (see Exit by stated obligation).
- **Walk-out.** A quarrel ends with an order to leave, a declaration that the meeting is over, or the speaker leaving with a parting demand (see Eviction and walk-out).
- **Ritual.** Calls end with a run of short formulas: a request to call back, an offer to let the other go, exchanged love, a be-careful wish; visits end with regards to the family, a standing offer of help or a promise of lunch (see Exit by stated obligation and Standing offer of help).
- **Stacked assent.** A conversation is wrapped with repeated yeahs and all-rights once the matter is treated as closed (see Minimal replies and back-channels).
- **Pointed promise.** A polite agreement to meet again carries a remark that signals offense (see Veiled threat).

## Moves

The moves are the readers' own, merged across the seasons where they describe the same thing under different names, and ordered by how many episodes they were seen in. Episodes counts the episodes each season's readers logged the move in, added up across the seven season files; where one season logged it under more than one name, only the largest of those counts is used, so every figure is a floor. Who names the speakers the readers credited most often, and a rate is given where the construction was also measured in the word counts. The example exchanges are invented.

### 1. Echo challenge

- **Built:** Repeat the key word, figure, name or excuse from the other person's last turn as a bare question or in a flat tone, sometimes behind a scornful interjection or followed by a why, and then knock it down, demand where it came from, or let the echo stand as the refusal. It shows disbelief, buys time, sets up a counterattack or puts the word itself on trial; a variant echoes the whole question and rejects its premise, and a creditor throws a debtor's softening word straight back. An order to get out or a stock vulgar phrase can stand in for the echo, meaning the other cannot be serious.
- **Who:** Everyone. Tony most, then Christopher, Carmela, Meadow, Janice and Paulie; Melfi uses the same device as a reflection.
- **Episodes:** 72, logged as common in every season.
- **Rate:** the bare question word plus a challenge, 169 (0.39 per 1,000 words); the denial echo that repeats a word inside a question, 7 (0.02).
- **Example:**
  > A: It's only a small refactor.
  > B: Small? You touched forty files.

### 2. Sarcastic thanks, praise and agreement

- **Built:** Meet a letdown, a jab or an obvious lie with thanks, congratulations, a welcome, sympathy or agreement that plainly means the reverse, sometimes restated in an absurd absolute form, sometimes inflated into mock formality, and then deliver the real jab. Variants: an apology in a sneering tone that turns into an insult, mock permission that dares the other to do worse, a welcome that points out lateness, a sugary pet name in reply to anger, a sarcastic wish for the other's privileges. An unwanted arrangement can be mocked by describing it as a romance.
- **Who:** Tony and Carmela most, then Christopher, Meadow, Janice and Livia.
- **Episodes:** 38.
- **Example:**
  > A: I pushed straight to main, no review.
  > B: Beautiful. A real gift to all of us.

### 3. Therapist's reflection and reframe

- **Built:** Answer with interpretation rather than content. Restate the feeling in neutral clinical words, link it to an earlier remark or to childhood, name a pattern or a defense in one word, turn a question back with a why-now or a what-do-you-want, keep the client talking with one-word prompts, and steer back to his own experience when he talks about others; a session can end on a directive or homework.
- **Who:** Melfi above all; also Elliot, Sam, Sandy and the therapists of the later seasons. A rival boss's son borrows the move once in a sit-down.
- **Episodes:** 37.
- **Example:**
  > A: The deploy failed again and nobody on the team cares.
  > B: Nobody. You sound alone with it. When did that start?

### 4. Mock title or nickname

- **Built:** Rename someone, present or absent, after a celebrity, a cartoon, a royal or church rank, a food, a body feature or the flaw just shown, often tagged with a place word when he is in the room; puns on a surname or a trade belong here. Arrivals are hailed with the mock title before any greeting, and an objection to a nickname is waved off and the name kept.
- **Who:** Tony most, then Christopher, Carmela, Paulie, Meadow, Junior and Johnny Sack.
- **Episodes:** 33.
- **Example:**
  > A: I color-coded the whole backlog.
  > B: Everybody, bow. The Duke of Spreadsheets.

### 5. Counter-accusation

- **Built:** Do not answer the charge. Pin the same or a worse fault on the accuser (their own habit, the benefit they quietly took, where they were when it started), throw the insult back in the same words, point at someone else who did the same and got a better deal, or question the accuser's state of mind or motive. A demand can be met with a formal counterclaim, a dismissed weakness with the same weakness exposed in someone the critic reveres, and a therapist's interpretation with a question whether she is joking.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, Christopher, Meadow and A.J.; Melfi turns a charge back in session.
- **Episodes:** 32.
- **Example:**
  > A: You broke the nightly build.
  > B: Me? Who merged untested code all of last month?

### 6. Coded reference

## Speech acts

Each reader filed a note on all seven acts for every episode. The episode figures below count the 86 episode reports that recorded the act; the descriptions merge the seven season summaries.

### Threats

- **Seen in:** all 86 episodes, though in 7 of them the readers found no open threat said to a rival's or a listener's face, only veiled, planned or family ones.
- **How it is done:** Open threats are rare among the principals; most menace is indirect, conditional or delegated. The commonest grammar is a bare two-clause conditional with the if dropped, or an imperative followed by an or-clause, ending in a body part, a beating, jail or a vague sorrow. Harm is painted as a concrete picture (often mirroring an injury already done) or dressed as something else: hospitality, worry about the target's knees, car or children, advice about what the speaker would do in the listener's place, a question whether he understands, asking nicely, a remark that the next step will not be talk. Bosses keep their own hand out of view, citing unnamed people, agentless passives, or restraint framed as what they ought to do. Killings are ordered in euphemism rather than threatened. Leverage threats use exposure, cut supplies, debts with deadlines and interest, and from federal agents, charges and decades in prison, delivered politely. Inside families threats are hyperbole that nobody takes literally, and targets answer with dares.
- **Who:** Tony; Junior in agentless, rule-like terms; the New York side, Phil above all, more openly; Christopher in rages; Paulie; federal agents.

### Requests and orders

- **Seen in:** all 86 episodes.
- **How it is done:** Bare imperatives dominate in every episode among family and crew: doubled, chained without a conjunction, cut to one word or a noun, sharpened with profanity, capped with a time word or a curt go, rounded off with an okay or all-right tag, or carrying an overt you that makes the order pointed. Bosses also order through want-statements, gotta-obligations, rulings stated as future fact, make-sure clauses and orders relayed as what the boss wants, and they check that the order landed. Downward requests are blunt; upward or sideways ones are hedged with chains of if, could and maybe, framed as favors, shrunk to something small, padded with reasons and apologies for bothering, preceded by a gift, or pinned to a family occasion or a debt the other side owes. Suggestion questions serve as soft suggestions and as hostile commands. Women with children and help, officials, agents and therapists use polite modal forms, whose courtesy often leaves no room to refuse. Drink and food orders are bare noun phrases. Parents order through deals, conditions and formal house rules, and escalate by repeating the order and demanding it be said back.
- **Who:** Tony most; Carmela with the children; Johnny Sack and the agents with want-statements; therapists with polite directives.

### Refusals

- **Seen in:** all 86 episodes.
- **How it is done:** Among the men refusals are mostly flat and short: a single no, a repeated no, a casual nah, a curse, a profane two-word rejection, an image of the speaker's own death, or a formula declaring the subject closed, and repeated refusals harden with negative concord. Polite declines pair thanks or praise with a concrete or vague reason (already ate, a long drive, homework, a sick pet) and a promised next time, often without naming the real reason. Tony avoids refusing outright: he defers, promises to look into it, lists obstacles and then denies having said no, pushes the decision back onto the asker, or dodges a dangerous invitation with a boast; bosses relay denials through go-betweens or point to someone else's authority. Professionals and officials refuse in policy language with a regret formula and a rule. Money, food and gifts are refused as a ritual and then accepted. Harder refusals use sarcasm, an absurd alternative, a dare, or an offer reclassified as a fixed position.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, Melfi, Adriana with the agents, Phil and Paulie declining promotions.

### Apologies

- **Seen in:** 85 of 86 episodes; none was found in s06e20.
- **How it is done:** Short, functional and often hollow. Most come from below (a beaten debtor, staff, juniors after a dressing-down) and are often extracted under pressure. The usual shape is a quick sorry, often with an address term, plus a reason attached at once (traffic, a late call, worry, clients), a but that pivots to self-defense, or a swerve to praise or business. Sorry also serves as the preface to a refusal, as bare sympathy, and as an apology for swearing in formal company. Many are conditional on offense being taken, made on someone else's behalf, sarcastic, cut off with a wave and a me-too, or replaced by a gift, money or a promise to cover costs. Tony seldom apologizes plainly; the fullest apologies come at moments of collapse, and a stacked string of apology formulas can be followed at once by a fresh insult. Several characters pointedly never apologize.
- **Who:** Subordinates, staff and the young most readily, Christopher among them; Melfi promptly and professionally; Tony rarely.

### Condolences

- **Seen in:** 57 of 86 episodes; in the other 29 the readers recorded none, or only passing sympathy for illness, injury or trouble.
- **How it is done:** Brief, formulaic and quickly redirected. The core is sorry plus the mourner's name, an endearment or the dead relative, a claim to know the feeling or to have been close, stock praise that the dead man was good, an offer of help or a standing invitation to call, answered with bare thanks or thanks for coming. Groups toast the dead with an Italian blessing repeated in unison, and a blessing is attached to the dead man's name. Stock consolations recur: he did not suffer, everyone's time comes, at least it was quick, grief takes time. Food is the main currency (a rota of dishes, casseroles with heating instructions), along with flowers, donations and visits. Sympathy is often cut with business, money owed, jokes about the manner of death or the speaker's own concerns; an enemy's stiff, elaborate formula lands as provocation; the bereaved sometimes reject the ritual, and tough love orders a mourner to get over it. Officials and professionals offer formal versions on behalf of a body.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, the wake crowds, Phil and Junior.

### Compliments and respect

- **Seen in:** all 86 episodes.
- **How it is done:** Respect is performed through address and ritual more than through praise: title and surname from outsiders, staff and agents; title and first name from household help; the full first name from elders, wives and rival bosses; clipped names and initials from subordinates; honorifics for judges, priests and elders; deference formulas before disagreement; serving and deferring to the boss first; gifts of cash, wine, watches and jewelry. Praise between men is clipped, two or three words, counted in earnings, nerve, loyalty and silence under pressure, and often boosted with profanity or wrapped in a jab. Compliments go to looks, food, cooking, houses, clothes, gifts and children, and to the dead as legends. Much praise is instrumental or double-edged: flattery before a request, a compliment to a juror's family that works as a threat, backhanded credit, sarcastic titles. Thanks often take the form of praise for the giver's whole character; plain sincere praise is rare and stands out.
- **Who:** Tony, Carmela, the crew toward Tony, and outsiders and staff through titles.

### Insults and disrespect

- **Seen in:** all 86 episodes.
- **How it is done:** Profane insult is constant among the men: anatomical and sexual epithets as labels and as vocatives, stacked with modifiers in fury, with gendered insults for women. Ethnic, racial and anti-gay slurs appear casually and mostly pass without comment; a few are challenged. Bodies are the favorite target (weight above all, then noses, height, hair, teeth and age), followed by intelligence, sanity, class and a man's past role. Mock titles, celebrity and cartoon nicknames, puns on surnames and food words do much of the work, along with sarcasm, rhetorical questions guessing at someone's identity, and third-person mockery to the target's face. Insults are traded behind a man's back once he leaves or cannot follow the language, and an insult about a mother or a sister means the joking is over. Disrespect also works by omission and ritual: refusing a drink, not offering a seat, thin turnout at a wake, an honorific thrown back with contempt. Between friends, affectionate obscene names blur into teasing and are answered in kind.
- **Who:** Tony, Junior, Christopher and Paulie; Livia's disrespect is pointed and domestic.

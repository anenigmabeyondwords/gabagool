# Character voices

This file describes how each of the 29 characters with voice notes in at least eight of the 86 episodes talks, in order of how many episodes carry those notes (ties go to the character the season notes credit more often), from Tony Soprano down to Charmaine Bucco. It was built from a full read of every episode, consolidated into seven season notes, plus word counts over the whole corpus; each "By the numbers" gives episode coverage, how often the season notes credit the character with a construction, a move, a term or a malapropism, and corpus counts for the names other people use for them, and now also figures measured per speaker on a speaker-labeled copy of the episodes (audio diarization plus naming, which puts a name on 90% of the speech): words, turns, question rate, and the habits that are more theirs than the cast's. Each section also gets a short note on what makes the character funny. It contains no quoted dialogue: words from the show appear only as single terms or expressions of three words or fewer in the term, form and said columns, every example sentence is invented, and the slurs the notes record are described, never listed.

### What makes them funny

Switches registers mid-sentence, from menace to wounded little boy to consumer complaint. He mangles prestige words and insists he said them right, mocks therapy while weaponizing its vocabulary, and keeps the sharpest ledger in the room. His best laughs are a flat one-liner after catastrophe and a full sit-down over a newspaper, a haircut or juice pulp. He moralizes about exactly what he does every day and can't take a joke at his own expense.

### How they talk

His default turn is short and blunt: declaratives, bare imperatives, clipped questions with the auxiliary or the subject gone, and verbless fragments, usually opened by a pivot word, a name or an announcement that he is about to explain something, and closed with a tag that asks for agreement. He stretches into long chained runs only in therapy, in stories about his father and his childhood, when bargaining or persuading, and in tirades about everything his work pays for. Profanity is constant punctuation in any slot, softened to a minced form in front of his wife, his children and his therapist in the early seasons. Crime never gets its own name: he relabels it in business, medical, legal or union vocabulary, speaks of killing through euphemism, coded nouns and unnamed third parties, and on the phone cuts off anyone who says too much. Sarcasm is his reflex under attack (mock thanks, mock titles from films, cartoons and celebrities, absurd guessed answers to his own questions, puns right after violence), and when he wants weight he reaches for formal, legal, corporate or clinical words that misfire often enough to give him more malapropisms than anyone. With children, animals, grieving men and women he cares about he softens into pet names, flat repeated reassurance, compact fatherly maxims, cash and food; with his mother, his sister, rivals and officials he escalates fast into stacked obscenities and ethnic and anti-gay slurs.

### Their moves

- **Launder the business.** Crime is described as ordinary commerce, a medical matter or union routine; a killing is approved in a short euphemism or a two-word order.
- **Deflect the question.** A counter-question about the asker's motive or credentials, a flat claim of not knowing, a sudden change of subject, a deadpan literal answer, or a cover story repeated against every skeptic.
- **Ask it, then answer it.** A sarcastic opener followed by his own absurd guess: "What, the router just got tired? Took a nap?"
- **Rule by repetition.** He repeats an order until it is obeyed, rejects a bare answer, and makes the listener say aloud that he understood: "Say it back to me. Who resets the router?"
- **Hand it down and keep clear.** Decisions go to the captains, the chain of command is cited, and any fallout belongs to someone else.
- **Threaten by forecast.** A concrete picture of next time, framed as restraint or obligation: "Next time the tests are red on Friday, you're the one staying the weekend."
- **Pay instead of apologize.** He offers cash or a favor as settled fact, claims he had already decided whatever unwelcome news arrives, and when he does apologize it is sideways, sealed with an oath on God or his mother.
- **Resist the therapy, then blurt.** He mocks the jargon, throws the therapist's words back, demands plain words and practical steps, ends the session himself, deflects onto weather or food, and then tells the real story in one long run.
- **Close the door.** Absolutes and set formulas: a subject banned in his house, a matter declared fixed, the same sentence repeated until a plea dies, a two-word exit.
- **Praise, then the ask.** Late on, he flatters a person's reliability, profession or neutrality before steering to money.

### Across the seasons

In season 1 he deflects with jokes and announced refusals and guards therapy as a secret; his talk grows more confrontational with the therapist and his mother, flattens into depressed fragments, and ends with blunt talk about his mother's betrayal and an admission of therapy to the crew. Season 2 circles his mother, his sister and the therapy he refuses, then fills with long self-justifying speeches, and ends in catastrophizing and curses at his therapist. Season 3 is clipped orders and threats by forecast while therapy talk grows longer and more figurative, ending in profane commands mixed with borrowed formal and therapeutic words whenever he wants to sound principled. Season 4 runs from money harangues to managerial euphemism, a full apology when he quits therapy, and a defensive finale of denials and oaths at home. Season 5 moves from clipped control to confessional therapy and an official, speech-making register for his captains. In season 6a the coma dream gives him a mild, courteous standard English that vanishes when he wakes; medical vocabulary and talk of luck then wear back down into sarcasm and cost talk. In season 6b birthday jokes and mentoring speeches give way to confessed relief in therapy after Christopher's death, then to the clipped orders, code and repeated assurances of the war.

### What makes them funny

Suburban propriety and piety laid over full knowledge of what pays for it. She is the household's sharpest needle, with sarcastic deflations and hyperbolic escalation, and she bargains with God, schools and her husband like a union rep. In a crisis she turns to logistics and cured meats, and she keeps a domestic ledger as precise as any capo's.

### How they talk

Her sentences are complete, organized and well formed, more formal than the men's even in anger, with uncontracted negatives for firm refusals and the occasional hypercorrect form. In company she runs a hostess register: thanks, compliments, endearments, coffee and food pressed on guests, condolence dishes, praise for a host or a gift. At home she turns sharp: challenges that open with a one-word question, sarcastic permission, stacked rhetorical questions, her husband's bare name used as a brake, and counter-accusations that list what critics quietly took from the man they criticize. She prepares her case, asks permission to speak, concedes a point and reasserts her complaint in the next clause, and backs herself with outside authority: a magazine study, church teaching, a cousin's financial advice, her exam studies, therapy vocabulary. With the children she lays down deals, curfews and lists of bans, uses full names and a stiff prohibitive frame, and polices their language. She swears rarely, preferring religious oaths and minced forms, and apologizes for her language in formal company, until anger breaks through. She passes news along in reported chains of named sources.

### Their moves

- **The ladder to an ask.** Small talk and praise of her child, a pretext visit with a homemade dish, a check on whether she is being too forward, then a flat statement of what she wants, repeated word for word.
- **Counter-accusation by inventory.** She lists the gifts, favors and money the critic accepted from the very arrangement being criticized.
- **Bare-name rebuke and tautology.** Her husband's first name alone stops a remark; a proposition said twice closes a debate.
- **Flag it, then deliver it.** Bad news is announced as bad before it comes, and gossip arrives through a chain of who told whom.
- **Hurt shown sideways.** She says it is nothing, then names the slight, then refuses the offered fix.
- **Terms and conditions.** Permission and reconciliation come only with explicit conditions, curfews and non-negotiable terms; in bargaining she lets Tony keep offering until the price is clear.
- **Self-reproach.** She asks whether she is a bad parent and blames herself before anyone else can.
- **Food as care.** A plate is offered even in the middle of an argument.
- **Set the record straight, then take some back.** She defends Tony to outsiders, then honestly withdraws part of the defense.
- **Shrink the outburst.** After losing her temper she apologizes and recasts it as venting or being emotional.

### Across the seasons

Season 1 widens her vocabulary of feeling and self-worth, from sarcastic jabs and religious threats to confessions, therapy-style confrontations and an open case for leaving, with a few obscene outbursts late on. Season 2 pushes Tony toward therapy, campaigns for Meadow's college and turns flirtatious and pretext-making during a near-affair, ending at her sharpest at home and most protective in public. Season 3 moves from tennis-club politeness to open moral reckoning with psychiatrists and a priest, and ends with flat orders to her daughter. Season 4 runs from careful requests about money and estate planning to guarded confession about Furio and a finale where, for the first time, she curses as freely as Tony. Season 5 fights over bills with sarcasm and self-blame, turns literary and theological during the affair, then takes up lawyers' and therapists' vocabulary and negotiates Tony's return in businesslike terms. Season 6a swings between hospital logistics and confessional speech about complicity and sin, then opens up in Paris. Season 6b goes from hostess formulas and money fights to soft comfort around A.J.'s depression and Christopher's death, and ends in brisk evacuation logistics.

### What makes them funny

The straight woman whose single dry sentence or flat literal echo punctures self-pity. She occasionally flashes pride over small things, curses offstage, and slowly picks up her patient's idiom. She is funniest calmly handing his own words back to him.

### How they talk

In session she speaks in complete, standard, measured sentences with clinical and Latinate vocabulary and almost no profanity or dialect features. Most of her turns are short: open questions that name an emotion and ask why now or toward whom, you-mean restatements, one-word reactions, two-word noncommittal acknowledgments. She keeps track of his exact words and images and returns them later, ties an offhand remark to his mother, his father, his childhood or a dream, and floats interpretations as stacked hypotheses or as how something sounds. Probing questions get counter-questions rather than denials, personal remarks get no answer, and requests are refused with thanks, an apology and an institutional reason, then restated firmly when pressed. She uses his full first name to slow him down or just before a hard verdict, and her irony is dry and brief. Outside the office she is wry with her former husband, sardonic and defensive with her own therapist, and under threat or after the assault her register drops into swearing, chains of rhetorical questions and blame.

### Their moves

- **Reflect, reframe, redirect.** She restates the feeling in neutral clinical words, notices a pattern or a slip, offers a darker reading, and steers back to his family or his own experience.
- **Turn the question back.** A demand for her opinion becomes a question about what he wants or why he is asking.
- **Name the feeling in the room.** Attacked, she labels the emotion behind the outburst instead of answering its content.
- **Warn, then interpret.** She flags that a point is sensitive, or admits where her knowledge comes from, before offering it.
- **Hold the line.** Gifts, fees, gossip, extra money and treating his wife are all refused, and the boundary is restated when he pushes.
- **Close the hour.** A time call, praise for progress, and something to work on before next week.
- **Defend the work.** Told therapy has done nothing, she lists concrete clinical gains.
- **Concede, then qualify.** A point granted, then punctured with a that-being-said turn.
- **Sharpen the restatement.** Late on, she hands his view back in a harsher form and lets him own or reject it.

### Across the seasons

In season 1 she moves from neutral questions and cautious hedging to bolder, directive interpretations, ending with a named diagnosis of Tony's mother, and when her own safety is threatened her composure gives way to swearing and bargaining. Season 2 opens with tight, guarded refusals, settles back into a calm clinical register, and grows more direct about fear and sadness, while outside the office she curses and drinks. ### What makes them funny

Entitled, literal-minded and aimless. He misreads poems and his own family, discovers existential dread or geopolitics at the most inconvenient moment, and makes escalating plans and cover stories that collapse on contact with reality.

### How they talk

His turns are short bursts: a single word, a bare denial, a claim of not knowing, a vague later. Teen slang runs through every season, along with like as a hedge and a quotative and trailing general extenders. He hunts loopholes and bargains (extra time, the car right away, television before homework), protests unfairness, and turns a parent's question back with a sarcastic like-clause, a what-am-I question, a defiant one-word comeback or a self-pitying rhetorical question. He picks up big words half understood and mixes them up: philosophers and a rapper as a boy, clinical, political and military vocabulary later. Gifts, cars, clubs and champagne bring out gushing, brand names and exact prices. Cornered, he narrates in run-on chains that spread the blame to unnamed others or to his parents' choices, minimizes in stages, and recasts misconduct as principle; from his teens on he swears back at his parents and labels people with slurs.

### What makes them funny

A precocious daughter who fires seminar vocabulary and literary critics at her parents and deflates them in one line, then reflexively recites the family cover story or defends due process when the defendant is family.

### How they talk

She is quick, articulate and direct, keeps full auxiliaries more often than the adults around her, and hedges less as she grows up. Two registers run side by side: teen and college slang (flat dismissals, intensifiers, discourse like, a sarcastic emphatic negative) and an elevated academic, clinical, legal and political vocabulary she uses to puncture her parents. With them she is brief and firm: an address word plus a complaint, one-word replies, pleas to drop a subject, comments on the conversation itself that close it down, mock titles, their own principles quoted back, and direct charges of racism and hypocrisy. She defends herself with a flat denial and a chain of other people's actions, refuses by stacking regretful excuses rather than saying no, and argues from seniority, statistics, age and adult rights. The plain word for her father works as a brake and a summons; the childish one comes out when she is frightened or wants something. With other people's parents, her grandmother and in formal introductions she is polite, gushing and complete.
